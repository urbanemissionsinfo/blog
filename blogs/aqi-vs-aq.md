# Absolute Concentrations Matter More Than the AQI

Clear distinction between the scientific data and the communication index is essential for effective environmental monitoring.
![alt text](blogs/aqi-vs-aq.jpg)

The Air Quality Index (AQI) is a calculated metric designed for public communication. The methodology typically requires data from at least six pollutants: PM2.5, PM10, SO2, NO2, CO, and ozone. An index is calculated independently for each pollutant based on its health impact ratings and pre-determined break points, and the highest value among them is designated as the AQI for that day, identifying the specific "sub-index" pollutant responsible.

While useful for simplifying complex data for the general public, the AQI can be manipulated if a city does not report all six pollutants. For instance, if a city focuses heavily on dust management and only reports PM10 data, the resulting AQI may appear improved even if PM2.5 levels remain high. This lack of transparency can create a misleading representation of air quality.

In contrast, absolute concentration values -- for example, in micrograms per cubic meter -- provide a scientific and transparent baseline. Unlike the AQI, which varies in methodology between countries, absolute units are universal and permit direct global comparisons. 

Relying on these raw numbers reduces the risk of manipulation and ensures a more accurate understanding of the trends of all the pollutants affecting our health.
